# How consultants actually get clients, and what that means for the first post

*Research, 11 Oct 2026. Sources at the end. Figures from vendor blogs are marked as such.*

## 1. Where the work comes from

- **Referrals are the main channel, by a distance.** Across consultants, about 60% of business arrives through referrals; 31% of consultants get 60–80% of their work that way and 19% get 80–95% (Consulting Success survey, widely cited). Asked for their most powerful channel, 63% say networking and referrals; social media is second at 25%.
- **Referrals come from visibility, not luck.** In Hinge's professional-services research, 81.5% of firms had received a referral from someone who was never a client. Those "expertise referrals" trace back to speaking (30%), articles (20%), social media (17%) and books (15%). Only 8% of consultants spend most of their marketing time on referrals, which is the gap to exploit.
- **Buyers screen you before they ever talk to you.** 51.9% of buyers have ruled a firm out without a conversation (Hinge). Your profile, site and last ten posts are that screening.
- **Nearly nobody is shopping today.** Edelman–LinkedIn's B2B study (about 3,500 decision-makers, seven countries incl. India, fielded Dec 2023) frames it as 95% of buyers not actively looking at any given moment. 73% say an organisation's thought-leadership content is a more trustworthy basis for judging its capabilities than its marketing material. Secondary summaries of the 2025 edition add that 54% were prompted by such content to research something they weren't considering, and 55% use it to vet firms. Seven in ten decision-makers also say half or more of the thought-leadership they read offers no insight, so weak content costs trust.
- **The closest first-person account to your situation** is Jason Liu, an AI consultant who built his pipeline from writing about client work: 4–5 quality inbound leads a month, a 50% close rate, one $140k three-month deal from a founder who had read his posts. His rule: "Your content is often the first interview with a potential client." His method: record client calls (with permission), pull out the recurring questions, write up the answer with anonymised examples and your reasoning. And: "Contractors sell tasks. Consultants sell outcomes."

## 2. What they post

Practitioner consensus (no controlled study exists; the ranking below is what consultant-marketing guides and first-person accounts agree on):

| Post type | What it does | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **One lesson from a real situation**, plain language, the lesson stated clearly | Draws the highest density of *qualified* DMs, because it self-selects readers with the same problem. One per week. | Consultant guides; Jason Liu's method |
| **Client result (case study)**: baseline → what was done → outcome, with permission | The most reliable lead post type; readers see their own situation in it | B2B vendor data, consultant guides |
| **Data plus your own view** | Builds authority; converts weakly on its own | Edelman (data-backed point of view wanted), guides |
| **Specific lead magnet** ("the 12-question production-readiness checklist") | Turns readers into a list of hand-raisers | Lead-gen guides |
| **Direct offer** | Only after the others have built trust | Consultant guides |

Market-statistics posts without a personal lesson are the weakest of these for leads. They attract general readers and other practitioners, not buyers.

## 3. How they run it

- **Cadence:** 2–4 posts a week is now optimal, down from 5–6 (van der Blom, 2026 report, 1.3M posts from 50k creators). Two to three is enough for most people.
- **Formats:** his working mix is text + image, a carousel, and another text + image. Polls get reach but not engagement. Standalone LinkedIn articles get ~0.4x normal reach; newsletter editions convert best of anything on the platform.
- **Distribution is now an interest graph, not a relationship graph.** Your comments on other people's posts shape who sees yours. Commenting substantively on 5–10 posts a day in your topic is as important as posting.
- **Links:** a link in the body reduces median reach by about 19% (secondary report of van der Blom's data); put links in the first comment.
- **Profile as the sales page:** headline that states the offer, Featured section with the site and one case study, recent posts visible. Visitors who find no recent posts leave.
- **Time horizon:** pipeline from posting takes 3–6 months. Nobody reports leads from a single post.

## 4. What this means for you

1. **The first post is for the people who already know you.** About 600 of your connections are ex-colleagues, batchmates, students and co-founders. They are the referral engine, and most of them do not know what you build now. The post's job is to tell them, in one concrete lesson, so they can repeat it to someone with a problem.
2. **Write about what you did, not about the market.** The anonymised lesson is the format with the best evidence, and it needs no client or product name. Jason Liu's whole pipeline was built on exactly that.
3. **Do the warm outreach in the same week.** List 50 people who know your work. Send each a two-line message: what you build now, and one question about their world. This is the highest-yield action in the research, and it is private.
4. **Set the cadence, not the campaign.** Two posts a week for twelve weeks, one lesson and one case or data post, plus daily comments on founders' and operators' posts in your target industries. Judge it at week twelve, not week one.
5. **Build the lead magnet by week three.** A one-page "production-readiness checklist" (the questions your Audit asks) is the natural one; it turns comments into a list.

## 5. The first post (v4): one lesson, no names

```
The most useful line in any AI assistant's prompt is the one that tells it to stop.

Gartner expects over 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027. The reasons it lists are cost, unclear value and weak risk controls. Not wrong answers. Wrong behaviour when there is no good answer.

A pattern I've seen more than once, in more than one industry:

The demo answers every question. The production system has to refuse some.
An assistant in a regulated field has to hand a red-flag case to a person instead of improvising.
A ticketing agent has to recognise the ticket it already filed instead of filing it twice.
A phone agent has to say it didn't catch the answer instead of recording a guess.

None of that is model work. It is a guardrail, a duplicate check, a confidence threshold, and a fallback for the day the model API returns 503. Boring parts. They are also the parts that decide whether the team still trusts the system in month three.

So the build order I've settled on: the stop conditions first, the evaluation set second, the clever prompt last.

If you run an assistant in production: what happens in yours when the model times out?
```

About 200 words. No client, product or employer. One sourced statistic. The three examples are generic situations, not your projects.

**The closing question** is deliberate and self-selecting. Only people who run an assistant in production can answer it, and those are the people who buy a Production-Readiness Audit. Expected replies: "it retries", "we fall back to a smaller model", "the user sees an error", "honestly, we don't know". Each one is a conversation you can continue in the comments and, if it goes well, in a DM with the Calendly link. If you prefer no question, end at "the clever prompt last."

## 6. The warm-outreach message (send to 50 people, one at a time)

```
Hi [name], it's been a while since [where you overlapped]. Quick update from my side: for the last four years I've been building AI systems that have to work after the demo (agents, RAG, voice, with the guardrails and cost tracking around them), across healthcare, recruitment, legal and support. I put the work in one place: www.srinivasdharavath.com. What are you working on these days?
```

No ask. The question at the end starts the conversation; the referral comes later, from them.
